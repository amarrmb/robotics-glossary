**Robot Dynamics** is the study of the *forces and torques* required to produce motion in a robot — accounting for mass, inertia, gravity, and friction. Where [kinematics](/robotics-glossary/concepts/fundamentals/kinematics/) asks **where** the robot is and how it moves geometrically, dynamics asks **what forces and torques** are needed to make it move that way. It is the difference between knowing a path exists and knowing how hard each motor must push to follow it.

<Aside type="tip">
Kinematics is the geometry of motion; dynamics is the *physics* of motion. If you ignore dynamics, your robot's arm will droop under its own weight, overshoot when it speeds up, and fight gravity it never accounted for.
</Aside>

## The Equation of Motion

Every rigid-body robot obeys one master equation, the **manipulator equation**. In plain terms:

> The torque you apply at each joint must supply three things at once: the torque to **accelerate** the links (their inertia resists speeding up), the torque to handle **velocity-dependent effects** (Coriolis and centrifugal forces that appear when joints move together), and the torque to **hold against gravity**.

Naming the symbols before the math:

- **q** — the vector of joint positions (angles or slides)
- **q̇** (q-dot) — joint *velocities*
- **q̈** (q-double-dot) — joint *accelerations*
- **M(q)** — the **mass/inertia matrix**: how much each joint resists acceleration, depending on the current pose
- **C(q, q̇)** — the **Coriolis & centrifugal** term: forces that arise from joints moving simultaneously
- **g(q)** — the **gravity** term: torque needed just to hold the pose still
- **τ** (tau) — the **joint torques**, what the motors actually deliver

Put together:

```
M(q)·q̈  +  C(q,q̇)·q̇  +  g(q)  =  τ
└─inertia─┘  └─Coriolis/centrifugal─┘ └gravity┘  └motors┘
```

## Forward vs Inverse Dynamics

These mirror forward/inverse kinematics, but for forces instead of geometry:

```
Forward dynamics:   τ (torques)  → [solve for q̈] → motion        (used in SIMULATION)
Inverse dynamics:   desired q,q̇,q̈ → [solve for τ] → torques      (used in CONTROL)
```

**Forward dynamics** answers "given these motor torques, how will the robot move?" — this is exactly what a physics simulator like **Isaac Sim** or **MuJoCo** computes every timestep. **Inverse dynamics** answers "to follow this trajectory, what torques do I command?" — the basis of gravity compensation and feedforward control. Model-based controllers (MPC, LQR) and any serious sim-to-real pipeline all need an accurate dynamics model; if the model is wrong, the simulation lies and the controller fights the real robot.

## Worked Example: Gravity Compensation on a Pendulum

Take the simplest case — a single revolute joint holding a point mass `m` at the end of a massless link of length `L`, at angle `θ` measured from vertical. To hold it *still* (no acceleration, no velocity), the inertia and Coriolis terms vanish, leaving only gravity:

```
τ = m · g · L · sin(θ)
```

With `m = 2 kg`, `L = 0.5 m`, `θ = 30°`, `g = 9.81 m/s²`:

```
τ = 2 · 9.81 · 0.5 · sin(30°)
  = 2 · 9.81 · 0.5 · 0.5
  = 4.905 N·m
```

So the motor must supply **4.905 N·m** just to keep the arm from falling — before any actual motion. A controller that adds this term as feedforward lets the [PID loop](/robotics-glossary/concepts/control/pid/) handle only the small errors, not the whole weight of the arm.

```python
import numpy as np

def gravity_compensation_torque(m, L, theta_deg, g=9.81):
    """Holding torque for a single-link pendulum (mass m at length L)."""
    return m * g * L * np.sin(np.radians(theta_deg))

tau = gravity_compensation_torque(m=2.0, L=0.5, theta_deg=30.0)
print(f"Holding torque: {tau:.3f} N·m")
# Output: Holding torque: 4.905 N·m
```
